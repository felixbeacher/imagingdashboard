Source definitions, dates and estimate limitations

The market-size figures are published by independent commercial research firms; their segment boundaries, reporting periods and proprietary calculations are not necessarily identical. Where MRI fits in to clinical care

Clinical context rather than patient-specific recommendations. Indications depend on the diagnostic question, safety screening, access, protocol and appropriateness guidance.

Brain and neurological disease

What it measures: CNS anatomy and selected tissue properties using T1, T2, diffusion and other sequences.

Why it matters for imaging: stroke, demyelination, epilepsy and tumour workups depend on sequence selection and specialist reporting.

Spine and musculoskeletal MRI

What it measures: marrow, intervertebral discs, ligaments, muscles, tendons and joints.

Why it matters for imaging: demand includes sports injuries, degenerative disease and infection, with long protocols and patient positioning influencing throughput.

Oncology and whole-body MRI

What it measures: structural, diffusion-weighted and selected contrast-enhanced characteristics of tumours.

Why it matters for imaging: tumour staging, therapy monitoring and protocol consistency influence exam length and service capacity.

Cardiovascular MRI

What it measures: ventricular function, myocardial tissue characteristics, blood flow and congenital anatomy.

Why it matters for imaging: specialist acquisition, ECG gating, patient cooperation and interpretation expertise are essential.

Abdominal and pelvic imaging

What it measures: liver, pancreas, biliary anatomy, bowel and pelvic organs, using organ-specific sequences.

Why it matters for imaging: prostate, breast, hepatobiliary and pelvic pathways require distinct protocols and sometimes contrast agents.

Paediatric MRI

What it measures: brain, spine and other soft tissues without ionising radiation.

Why it matters for imaging: motion control, child-friendly protocols and sometimes sedation affect scanner workflow and staffing.

Original clinical orientation: ACR Appropriateness Criteria. MRI requires implant/device screening, controlled magnetic-field access and protocol-specific safety assessment; it is not interchangeable with CT.

Technology and service considerations

Technology developments to track

Magnetic field strength and gradients1.5T and 3T are widely deployed clinical configurations, while low-field, ultra-high-field and dedicated MRI serve different clinical and research needs. Higher field strength alone does not imply better performance for every application.

Low-helium, sealed and smaller-footprint magnetsCooling design, installation requirements, servicing and operating costs can affect access and replacement decisions. Check manufacturer-specific claims and lifecycle costs.

Acquisition acceleration and reconstructionParallel imaging, compressed sensing and AI-enabled reconstruction can shorten examinations or affect image quality, but require validation for each protocol and clinical task.

MRI safety and patient experienceImplant/device compatibility, ferromagnetic object screening, acoustic noise, RF heating, claustrophobia and gadolinium-contrast considerations all influence service planning.

Safety reference: ACR MR Safety. This is background context, not a real-time safety indicator.

Sources, coverage and methodology

Detailed interpretation and update rules
  • OECD capacity is MRI scanners per million people. Examination activity is MRI scans per 1,000 people per year. Source definitions and coverage can vary by country and provider.
  • Each country in the selected region has its own colour. Published observations are shown as solid lines; sufficiently supported modelled extensions are dashed in the same colour, with sensitivity shading. Gaps remain gaps, and no regional mean or total is calculated.
  • MRI projections require four consecutive annual source observations ending in 2024 or later. A model fitted to the first three predicts the fourth as a one-year historical holdout; forecasts are withheld if the relative error exceeds 10% for scanner density or 15% for examinations. If the holdout is adequate, an annualised trend is fitted on all four readings, capped at ±5% and ±8% respectively. The widening ±2-percentage-point annual-growth sensitivity is not a statistical confidence interval, and the result is not an OECD forecast. Actual published readings always take precedence over modelled ones.
  • PubMed searches MRI-specific titles from the preceding 365 days and evaluates a recent candidate pool; case reports, editorials, reviews and non-X-ray tomography are excluded. Verified indexed publication types and specialist-journal relevance inform an editorial prioritisation that limits repeated topics. Displayed papers are a selected discovery sample, not a systematic review, validated quality ranking or measure of clinical benefit.
  • Data are retrieved at automated rebuild time; "retrieved" is not the date that a study or annual MRI observation was recorded. Any unavailable source is labelled as such.
