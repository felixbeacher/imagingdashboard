How the total is constructed

Each component is converted to a historical percentile using the same fixed January 2000–December 2025 reference window: 312 monthly observations per series. Score = 100 × (reference observations below the current value + half the reference observations equal to it) / 312. The existing calibration snapshot is frozen; future readings above its maximum can reach 100. A score of 90 means the underlying observation is higher than roughly 90% of the reference months. This is a percentile scale, not a z-score, and it does not measure a 90% chance of an adverse event.

Total global risk = (GPR score + financial stress score + supply-chain score + energy score + food score) / 5. All five have equal weight. The coloured areas contribute one-fifth of each component score and add exactly to the white total line; their colours match the component lines below. The CSV also includes the literal sum on a 0–500 scale.

Daily OFR readings are averaged by calendar month; other inputs are already monthly. All plotted months have all five inputs: there is no interpolation, forward filling or substitution for missing data. The reference window is retrospective, so this is a historical comparison rather than a real-time backtest. The 2026 update retains the existing calibration observations; updated source histories and source rounding can slightly change previous plotted readings. Freeze this reference when adding future observations; new scores can be calculated as 100 × (number of reference values below x + half the number equal to x) / 312. Recalibrating the reference would change historical scores.

Higher values always mean more of the selected pressure. Nominal food and energy prices are not adjusted for inflation. Equal weights are a transparent choice rather than an empirically validated model: correlated components can count related shocks more than once, and calm components can dilute a severe regional crisis. The composite itself is an average of percentiles, not a historical percentile of total risk.

What each measure tells you

Geopolitical tension — GPR

Caldara and Iacoviello’s monthly Geopolitical Risk Index measures the share of newspaper articles discussing adverse geopolitical events, threats, war and terrorism. It is the direct geopolitical component; higher coverage produces a higher score. It measures reported attention rather than troop movements, battlefield intensity or fatalities, and can miss under-reported conflicts. The original index is normalised to an average of 100 over 1985–2019. Source and methodology.

Financial stress — OFR FSI

The US Treasury Office of Financial Research combines 33 market variables covering credit, equity valuations, funding, safe assets and volatility, with US, other advanced-economy and emerging-market contributions. Daily observations are averaged within each month. A positive original reading means above-average stress; a negative reading means below-average stress. This broader global measure replaces VIX and STLFSI to reduce duplication. It responds to many non-geopolitical shocks and is not a measure of sovereign default risk alone. Source and methodology.

Supply-chain pressure — GSCPI

The New York Fed’s monthly Global Supply Chain Pressure Index combines transport costs and manufacturing delivery, backlog and inventory indicators across seven economies. Original values are standard deviations from average conditions; higher values indicate tighter supply chains. It captures bottlenecks more broadly than a single freight rate. Demand changes also affect it, and a local shipping crisis may have only a modest effect on the worldwide aggregate. This report uses the October 2026 release column, including revised history. Source and methodology.

Energy price pressure — World Bank Energy Index

The World Bank Pink Sheet’s monthly nominal US-dollar Energy Index aggregates energy commodity prices, with 2010 equal to 100. Higher prices are treated as greater pressure on energy importers and consumers. It replaces OVX and proprietary futures spreads with an openly licensed, long-running price measure. It is a pressure proxy: demand-led price increases also raise it, falling prices can hurt exporters, and neither volatility nor physical shortage is measured directly. Source and methodology.

Food price pressure — World Bank Food Index

The Pink Sheet’s monthly Food Index combines food commodity prices, with 2010 equal to 100, in nominal US dollars. Higher prices indicate greater international food affordability pressure. It replaces FAO’s index to keep food and energy within one documented, openly licensed source. It does not measure hunger, local retail prices, access to aid or harvest volumes; exchange rates and domestic distribution can produce severe food insecurity even when this index falls. Source and methodology.

11 March 2020 · COVID-19 pandemic

WHO characterised COVID-19 as a pandemic. Financial stress reaches its highest monthly reading in this chart in March 2020 (OFR FSI 6.123). The pandemic and associated economic disruption provide context for this spike; the declaration date alone is not a causal estimate. Supporting source.

December 2021 · Supply-chain peak

The downloaded GSCPI history peaks within the chart window at 4.43 standard deviations in December 2021. This marks the sustained pandemic-era logistics squeeze, rather than a single-day event. The broad pressure measure remained extreme before Russia’s full-scale invasion. Supporting source.

24 February 2022 · Russia invades Ukraine

Russia’s full-scale invasion is followed by a March 2022 GPR reading of 318.95 and the maximum in the constructed total (91.8/100). Food prices peak in May and energy prices in June in this dataset. These different timings illustrate transmission through commodity markets and supply constraints, rather than a simultaneous jump in every series. Supporting source.

10 March 2023 · Banking turmoil

Silicon Valley Bank closed on 10 March, followed by acute domestic and global banking stress. This event is retained as financial context, although the monthly OFR series here does not produce a new window maximum. Monthly averaging can conceal brief daily surges; this is a reminder that not every dramatic news event creates a dramatic monthly composite change. Supporting source.

7 October 2023 · Israel–Gaza war

The Hamas-led attacks on Israel and the ensuing war in Gaza coincide with a sharp rise in geopolitical news risk. The composite rises as several components are elevated, but the chart cannot assign a share of that rise to a particular actor or event. Supporting source.

January 2024 · Red Sea shipping disruption

Attacks on shipping and rerouting away from the Suez Canal disrupted trade. The IMF reported a 50% year-on-year fall in Suez trade in the first two months of 2024. January is a representative period marker, not the start date of the attacks. The global supply-chain index remains far below its pandemic peak, showing that a serious regional disruption need not recreate a worldwide bottleneck. Supporting source.

2 April 2025 · US tariff shock

The US tariff announcement and subsequent trade tensions generated renewed market turbulence. The IMF’s April 2025 financial stability assessment links the tightening of financial conditions to the tariff-related uncertainty. The OFR component rises in April, while weaker energy prices can offset part of the increase in the total. Supporting source.

13 June 2025 · Israel–Iran escalation

Israel’s strikes against Iran triggered an emergency Security Council discussion and a further escalation of regional conflict. The GPR component is very high in June. Monthly energy prices and the broader total need not move by the same amount: market expectations, other supply and demand forces, and the monthly averaging period all matter. Supporting source.

28 February 2026 · Iran escalation

US–Israeli strikes against Iran and subsequent Iranian retaliation across the region mark a major escalation. The following month, GPR reaches 330.18, the highest raw geopolitical reading in the displayed period. The broader composite rises but remains below its March 2022 peak: this is a descriptive association, not an estimate of the conflict’s causal effect. Supporting source.

The major risks — and the coverage gaps

Military escalation

Wars can spread through alliance commitments, retaliation, arms transfers and disruption of trade routes. Nuclear signalling and miscalculation can make consequences disproportionate to the initial incident. GPR covers news attention to these threats, but does not quantify the likelihood of a particular war or capture every ongoing conflict.

Financial contagion

Bank runs, funding shortages and sudden repricing can transmit political or economic shocks across borders. OFR FSI tracks realised market stress; low readings do not rule out hidden leverage, concentrated exposures or future crises.

Energy insecurity

Concentrated production, sanctions, infrastructure attacks and shipping chokepoints can disrupt access to oil, gas and coal. The Energy Index measures the price burden on importers. A supply interruption offset by weak demand may leave the index relatively calm.

Trade fragmentation and logistics disruption

Tariffs, export controls, shipping attacks and port closures can lengthen delivery times and encourage expensive rerouting. GSCPI captures widespread logistics pressure, but long-term fragmentation can reduce efficiency without causing a short-lived spike.

Sovereign debt and default

High debt-service costs, weak revenues and foreign-currency borrowing can force restructuring or fiscal retrenchment. OFR offers only indirect coverage. A dedicated sovereign component is omitted because the suggested CDS and EMBI products do not offer the clear, unrestricted redistribution basis sought here; their absence is a coverage gap, not evidence of low risk.

Currency instability and capital flight

Loss of confidence can weaken currencies, raise imported food and energy costs and drain reserves. Safe-haven inflows may tighten financing elsewhere. OFR captures some market spillovers, but this dashboard has no dedicated currency index; dollar-denominated commodity prices can understate local-currency pressure.

Food insecurity

Conflict, damaged infrastructure, export restrictions and weather shocks can constrain staple supplies. International food prices are one channel into household hardship. Income losses, local prices and interrupted aid can worsen hunger even as the global Food Index declines.

Cyber and digital infrastructure attacks

Attacks on payments, telecommunications, energy networks or public services can cause large cross-border losses. No cyber score is included: CISA KEV entries count recognised exploited vulnerabilities, not geopolitical incidents or their severity, and cataloguing changes would compromise comparisons over this six-year window.

Climate and extreme weather

Warming can amplify heat stress, drought, crop losses and infrastructure disruption, potentially aggravating existing political tensions. Climate is excluded from the chart and composite: monthly global temperature is a structural background indicator rather than a direct measure of acute geopolitical pressure. Local hazard exposure and resilience determine actual losses.

Political and institutional uncertainty

Elections, regulatory reversals, sanctions and trade disputes can delay investment and undermine cooperation. GPR and market stress cover some consequences, but a dedicated policy-uncertainty measure is absent. The composite should therefore not be presented as complete coverage of all ten original categories.

Selection and reuse

“Free to access” does not automatically mean free to republish. This selection uses openly licensed or government-produced published aggregates, with attribution and applicable conditions. It does not claim every underlying series is copyright-free. Cboe VIX/OVX, Baltic Dry, JPMorgan EMBI, Deutsche Bank CVIX and S&P GSCI were left out to avoid their proprietary redistribution questions. ACLED, EM-DAT and news-scraping measures were not needed once scope, consistency and reuse requirements were applied. FAO is a viable attributed open-data alternative; one food index is sufficient here.
